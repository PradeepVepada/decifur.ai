DISCUSSION 
The fluorographic 
images demonstrate 
that 
CAMP 
waves indeed propagate 
through 
the aggregating 
cell 
monolayer. 
The quantitation 
presented 
in this report 
gives detailed 
information 
on the dimensions 
and rel- 
ative intensities 
of these waves. Several conclusions 
can 
be derived from our analysis: 
(1) The dimensions and intensities 
of the CAMP waves 
were remarkably 
consistent 
for those taken from many 
independent 
regions within 
a g-cm-diameter 
field. This 
consistency 
allows a typical 
CAMP wave to be defined. 
The width 
of the peak at half-maximum 
is about 700 
pm and occupies about 43% (700 pm/1600 pm) of the 
peak-to-peak 
distance. 
(2) Annihilation 
zones, the regions in which two op- 
positely directed CAMP waves meet and extinguish, 
con- 
tain more CAMP than found on average along the CAMP- 
containing 
bands. However, 
it was puzzling 
that small 
patches appearing 
at random along the bands were also 
of higher 
intensity. 
(3) Evidence suggests that CAMP levels at the centers 
of the concentric patterns oscillate with a period of about 
5-7 min. The intensity 
of the bands near spiral centers 
frequently 
started 
at very low levels and increased 
to 
a plateau over a distance 
of about 2 mm. In counter- 
rotating 
spirals, the two origins joined to form a longer 
stretch of low intensity. 
(4) Overlays of the dark-field 
photograph 
of the cell 
monolayer 
and the fluorographic 
image of the CAMP 
waves which 
were carried 
out by computer 
analysis 
qualitatively 
agreed with 
those done by visual 
inspec- 
tion. These suggest that the regions of elongated 
cells 
correspond 
to those with elevated CAMP. 
In every fluorographic 
image examined 
the patterns 
of CAMP-containing 
bands matched identically 
the pat- 
terns of light bands on a dark background 
seen in dark- 
field photographs 
of the aggregating 
cell monolayers. 
Clearly the outward velocity of the regions of cell shape 
change is equal to the velocity 
of propagation 
of the 
CAMP waves. For the fluorograph 
analyzed 
in this re- 
port, that velocity 
was about 300 pm/min. 
Although 
it 
is appreciated 
that this velocity 
may vary for different 
waves in the field (Gross et al., 1976), the average value 
can be used to convert all radial 
distances to approxi- 
mate times. The conversion 
is simply: time = distance/ 
velocity. 
The width 
of the peak at half-height 
of 700 
pm, when corrected for diffusional 
spreading, 
suggests 
that the major part of the relayed CAMP signal lasts 
for 2.0 min (Table 1). The period between 
successive 
signals is about 5.5 min (no correction 
for diffusion 
is 
necessary here). These values are remarkably 
consis- 
tent with biochemical 
measurements 
of the time course 
of elevated CAMP levels in synchronized 
cell suspen- 
sions (Gerish and Wick, 1975; Shaffer, 1975; Roos et al., 
1975; Gerish et al., 1977; Geller and Brenner, 
1978; De- 
vreotes and Steck, 1979; Dinauer 
et al., 1980a-c).

Our statistical 
analysis 
indicates 
that 
higher 
CAMP 
concentrations 
are found within 
the annihilation 
zones, 
the regions 
where 
two oppositely 
directed 
CAMP waves 
meet. 
Since 
the concentration 
of extracellular 
CAMP 
attenuates 
dramatically 
with diffusion 
over a short 
dis- 
tance, 
the higher 
intensity 
in the annihilation

likely represents 
a stronger 
response 
of the cells in that

This finding 
indicates

A deli- 
cate balance 
between 
secretion

and phosphodies-

activity

(The cellular

saturates 
at an ex- 
tracellular

concentration 
of lo-” M CAMP.) 
As outlined 
previously, 
examination 
of concentric 
ter- 
ritories 
suggests 
that 
CAMP concentrations 
within 
the 
central 
cells oscillate 
periodically. 
Since 
the signaling 
time 
is about 
a third 
of the oscillation 
period 
(2 min/ 
6 min), 
concentric 
territories 
should 
have 
detectable 
CAMP at their 
centers 
in about 
a third 
of the cases. Of 
12 concentric 
centers 
examined, 
4 had elevated 
CAMP 
in the central 
region. 
These observations 
are consistent 
with those of spontaneous 
CAMP oscillations 
in shaken 
cell suspensions 
(Gerisch 
and Hess, 
1974) and suggest 
that 
centers 
consist 
of a cell or group 
of cells 
which 
oscillate 
spontaneously. 
However, 
the resolution 
of our 
technique 
is not sufficiently 
high to rule out that 
a few 
central 
cells continuously 
release 
CAMP (which 
we can- 
not 
detect) 
and 
trigger 
the 
nearby 
cells 
to oscillate 
(which 
we do detect). 
This seems 
unlikely 
based 
on the 
known 
properties 
of the response 
where 
continuous 
ap- 
plication 
of CAMP causes 
adaptation 
after 
several 
min- 
utes. 
It is more 
likely 
that 
a group 
of central 
cells os- 
cillate 
according 
to a mechanism 
based 
on the known 
properties 
of the response-adaptation 
system 
(Dinauer 
et al., 1980b). Mathematical 
models 
have 
been 
devised 
which account 
for both spontaneous 
oscillations 
and re- 
lay of CAMP signals 
through 
the monolayer 
(Goldbeter 
and Segel, 1977). Similar 
mathematical 
models 
also lead 
to spiral 
wave solutions 
in which 
the CAMP concentra- 
tion builds 
up over a distance 
of about 
one wavelength 
away from the tip of the spiral 
(Hagan 
and Cohen, 1981). 
These calculations 
are consistent 
with our measure- 
ments of CAMP concentrations 
which 
build 
up to a pla- 
teau within 
a few millimeters 
from the tip of the spiral. 
We have 
previously 
proposed 
that 
the leading 
edge 
of the wave peak provides 
the gradient 
which 
attracts 
cells toward 
the aggregation 
center 
(Tomchik 
and De- 
vreotes, 
1981). The radial 
distance 
spanned 
by the gra- 
dient 
is a function 
of the velocity 
of wave propagation 
and the kinetics 
of CAMP secretion. 
The measured 
wave 
velocity 
is approximately 
300 pm/min 
and the rate 
of 
CAMP secretion 
measured 
in isolated 
cells 
reaches 
a 
maximum 
after about 2.0 min. This results 
in a gradient 
that spans 
a radial 
distance 
of about 
700 pm. In theory, 
the same gradients 
could be formed 
only by alteration 
of both parameters 
simultaneously. 
For instance, 
were 
the wave velocity 
faster 
(or slower) 
but the kinetics 
of 
CAMP secretion 
also faster 
(or slower), 
the same 
gra- 
dient would be set up. Apparently, 
through 
evolutionary 
adaptation, 
the gradient-sensing 
mechanism 
has been 
closely 
integrated 
with 
both the kinetics 
of the signal- 
ing response 
and 
the wave 
velocity 
such 
that 
aggre- 
gation 
will be successful 
over a wide range 
of environ- 
mental 
conditions. 
The cell-to-cell 
relay 
of a chemical 
signal 
is a simple 
mechanism 
for a group 
of identical 
cells to communi- 
cate. All the cells in the monolayer 
have the same 
re- 
sponse 
(i.e., activation 
and 
subsequent 
adaptation 
of 
adenylate 
cyclase) 
to extracellular 
CAMP. Yet this 
is 
sufficient 
to spontaneously 
produce 
uniform 
chemical 
waves which 
propagate 
through 
the monolayer 
and or- 
ganize 
cellular 
aggregation. 
It is possible 
that 
similar 
mechanisms 
operate 
in other systems 
where form arises 
in a population 
of identical 
cells.
